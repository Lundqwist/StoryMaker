### Smoke over Thornridge

I was young that autumn, younger than I knew, and Elder Tavuk still called me *little ember* when he thought no one was listening.

It began with the wind. He sniffed it the way old shamans do, as if the air owed him an answer, and sent me up the ridge to the old watchtower. I remember the climb better than I should: the dust coyotes that would not leave me be, the scorpids in the red rocks, the smell of burning timber getting stronger with every step. Eight of them sat around the fire inside the tower, at least eight, warming their hands on wood that had been ours. I lay in the scrub and counted them twice, and I did not let them see me. I was proud of that, then.

Tavuk only nodded when I told him. *So. The spirits were right to be restless.* Then he put four smooth stones in my hands and sent me to the Four Springs.

> A shaman who cannot hear the earth is only an orc with a stick.

I set the stones where the water rose, one by one, and I listened. At the third spring a scorpid found me first, and I woke on the cold ground with the taste of iron in my mouth and the sky turning purple above me. I finished the fourth anyway. And there it was, under everything, a low hum like a drum heard through a mountain. I have heard it every day since. I did not have a word for it that evening. I only knew I was not alone any more.

---

The next night Grunt Morza wanted the ridge back.

I did not go up alone that time. A troll named Tikko, a hunter with a laugh like a cracked bell, fell in beside me without being asked, and I have never been so glad of a stranger. We went through the bandits like a storm through dry grass, and at the top of the watchtower their chief, Varga, stood with her back to the burnt wall and would not yield. I felt the earth answer me when I struck. I felt myself grow.

We brought Morza the torn banner, and he laughed until he coughed. *They'll sing about you at the fire tonight, Brakka. Badly, but they'll sing.*

They did. But first Old Hessa sent me to Redwater Bend for silverfin, and I sat on the bank with a line in the water while the stars came out, and I caught six fat ones and one old boot. I have told the story of the boot more often than the story of Varga. It is the better story.

Hessa gave me the first bowl, as she had promised. Tikko told the bandit story three times, and each time there were more bandits. By the third telling there were twenty, and I had fought half of them with my bare hands.

I let him tell it. Some nights, a story should be bigger than the truth.
